# ARTICLE 1

**Title:** Dog Scared of Fireworks? What Actually Helps (A UK Owner's Guide)
**Slug:** `dog-scared-of-fireworks-what-helps`
**Meta description:** 41% of UK dogs are frightened of fireworks. An honest guide to
what helps, what doesn't, and how to prepare before Bonfire Night — from a small UK shop.
**Publish:** August. **Target:** dog scared of fireworks what to do

---

If your dog spent last Bonfire Night shaking behind the sofa, you are very much not
alone. According to the PDSA's Animal Wellbeing Report, **41% of UK dog owners say
their dog is afraid of fireworks** — that's roughly 4.1 million dogs.

This guide is what we'd tell a friend. Some of it involves things we sell. Most of it
doesn't.

## Why fireworks are so much worse for dogs

Dogs hear roughly four times further than we do, and across a far wider range of
frequencies. A firework isn't just louder to them — it's louder, higher, and comes
from a direction they can't identify. Add the flashes and the smell of explosives and
you have an event with no pattern, no warning and no obvious escape.

That last part matters most. Fear that can't be escaped is what turns into panic.

## Start now, not on the night

This is the single most useful thing in this article: **most of what helps needs a
head start.**

A new bed, coat or snood introduced on 5 November is one more strange thing on an
already frightening night. The same item introduced in mid-October — worn for ten
minutes a day, on the sofa, with treats, when nothing scary is happening — becomes a
thing your dog already associates with calm.

Give yourself **two weeks minimum**. Four is better.

## What actually helps

**Build a den before you need it.** A covered, enclosed space in the quietest room —
under a table with a blanket over it, a crate with the door open and a duvet across
the top, a corner behind the sofa. Put their bed in it. Let them use it for weeks
before the fireworks. On the night, do not shut them in.

**Muffle, don't silence.** Close curtains, put the TV or radio on at a normal volume.
The aim isn't to drown the bangs out — it's to make them less sudden against the
background. A snood that covers the ears takes the edge off the sharpest sounds.

**Gentle, constant pressure.** A snug coat or wrap works on the same principle as a
weighted blanket for people: steady pressure is calming for many, though not all,
dogs. It isn't a treatment, and it doesn't work for every dog. It's worth trying
early enough to know whether yours is one of them.

**Give them a job.** Licking and sniffing are genuine self-soothing behaviours. A lick
mat with something spreadable frozen onto it, or a snuffle mat with their dinner
scattered through it, occupies the part of the brain that would otherwise be
scanning for the next bang. This is the cheapest and most reliably useful thing on
this list.

**Walk them early.** Get the walk done in daylight, well before anything starts. A
tired dog copes better, and you avoid being outside when the first one goes off.

**Feed them early too.** Many dogs won't eat once they're frightened.

**Sound desensitisation.** There are free firework-sound recordings on YouTube and the
Dogs Trust site. Play them at a volume so low your dog barely notices, paired with
something good, and increase over weeks. This genuinely works but it is slow — start
in September.

## What doesn't help

**Punishing or ignoring the fear.** The old advice that comforting a frightened dog
"reinforces" the fear is wrong. Fear is an emotion, not a behaviour — you cannot
reward it into existence. If your dog wants to be near you, let them.

**Forcing them out of their hiding place.** Behind the sofa is a legitimate coping
strategy. Leave them there.

**Over-the-counter "calming" supplements, bought on the night.** We don't sell these
and we're not qualified to advise on them. What we will say is that anything working
pharmacologically needs a vet's input, and buying an unfamiliar supplement the
afternoon before is the worst possible timing.

**Leaving them alone to "get used to it".** They won't.

## When to see your vet

If your dog's response goes beyond nervousness — self-injury, destroying doors,
losing bladder control, refusing to eat for a day afterwards — that's a clinical
noise phobia, and it's treatable. Your vet can help, and they'll want to see you in
September, not on 4 November. Nothing in this article is a substitute for that.

## A checklist for the night

- Walk and feed before dark
- Curtains closed, TV or radio on
- Den open and accessible, bed inside it
- Lick mat prepped and frozen earlier in the day
- Doors, windows, cat flaps shut — panicked dogs bolt
- Make sure their microchip details are up to date. More dogs go missing on 5
  November than any other night of the year
- Stay calm yourself. They read you constantly

### FAQ
**Do calming coats actually work?** For some dogs, noticeably. For others, not at all.
The mechanism — steady, gentle pressure — is the same idea as a weighted blanket.
It's a comfort tool, not a treatment, and the only way to know is to try it early.

**Should I get a puppy used to fireworks?** Yes. Sound desensitisation during the
socialisation window is far more effective than trying to undo a fear later.

**My dog was fine last year and terrified this year. Why?** Noise phobias often
develop or worsen with age, and one bad experience can be enough. It's common and
it's not something you did wrong.

# ARTICLE 2

**Title:** Settling a Rescue Dog: What the First Few Weeks Are Really Like
**Slug:** `settling-a-rescue-dog-first-weeks`
**Meta description:** An honest guide to the first days, weeks and months with a
rescue dog — the 3-3-3 rule, what's normal, and what actually helps them settle.
**Publish:** August. **Target:** settling a rescue dog first night

---

They've been home four days and they still haven't lain down properly.

That's not a problem with your dog and it isn't a problem with you. It's what the
first weeks look like, for almost everyone.

## The 3-3-3 rule (and why it's only roughly right)

The rescue world's rule of thumb: **3 days** to decompress, **3 weeks** to start
learning your routine, **3 months** to feel at home.

Treat it as a shape rather than a schedule. Some dogs settle in a fortnight. Some
take six months. A dog that's had several homes, or came from a difficult one, will
take longer — and there's usually a wobble around week three, just when you thought
things were going well.

## What's normal in the first days

- Not eating for 24–48 hours
- Pacing, or standing in doorways watching you
- Sleeping far more than you expected — or barely at all
- No interest in toys or play
- Following you from room to room, or hiding from you entirely
- Accidents indoors, even from a house-trained dog
- Shutting down completely — the "perfect" quiet dog who isn't relaxed, just overwhelmed

That last one catches people out. A dog who does nothing at all in week one is often
not calm; they're too shut down to do anything. It usually lifts.

## What actually helps

**Do less.** The instinct is to introduce them to everyone and show them everywhere.
Resist it. For the first fortnight: your house, your garden, quiet walks, you. No
visitors, no dog parks, no pet shop trips.

**Give them somewhere to hide, and don't follow them into it.** A bed with a raised
rim, a covered crate, a quiet corner. The point is that it's theirs. A dog with a
reliable escape route relaxes faster than one who has to stay alert.

**Be boringly predictable.** Same wake time, same feed time, same walk route. Routine
does more for a nervous dog in the first month than affection does.

**Don't force affection.** Let them come to you. Sit on the floor, look away, wait.
It can take days. It's worth it.

**Feed them something they have to work for.** A scatter feed in the garden, or dinner
in a snuffle mat, gives a nervous dog something to do that isn't scanning the room.
Sniffing lowers arousal — it's one of the few things that reliably does.

**Start alone-time early, in small doses.** Two minutes in another room, then five.
Building this in week two is far easier than fixing separation anxiety in month three.

## What to expect around week three

Many dogs get *worse* at about three weeks — more vocal, more testing, occasionally
the first growl. This is usually a good sign. They've stopped being too overwhelmed to
have opinions. Hold the routine, don't panic, and get a behaviourist involved if
anything frightens you.

## When to get help

Growling or snapping that escalates, destruction that risks injury, or a dog who
can't be left for two minutes without panicking — get a qualified, force-free
behaviourist involved early. It is much easier to change at week four than month six.

# ARTICLE 3

**Title:** Why Your Dog Panics When You Leave (And What Helps)
**Slug:** `dog-separation-anxiety-home-alone`
**Meta description:** Pacing, whining, chewing when you go out? An honest guide to
separation anxiety in dogs — what it is, what's just boredom, and what actually helps.
**Publish:** September. **Target:** separation anxiety dog home alone

---

The pacing starts when you pick up your keys.

## Boredom or anxiety? They need different fixes

This is the distinction that matters, and most advice skips it.

**Boredom** looks like: chewing that's focused on interesting objects, mess made
throughout the day, a dog who settles once given something to do, no distress on your
return.

**Separation anxiety** looks like: distress starting *before* you leave, focused on
exit points — doors, window frames, carpet by the door. Vocalising within minutes.
Drooling, panting, accidents from a house-trained dog. Frantic greeting on return.

Enrichment fixes boredom. It does not fix true separation anxiety — though it does
help around the edges.

## What helps with boredom

Feed them their breakfast out of something that takes twenty minutes instead of
eleven seconds. Rotate toys so they're novel. Leave a lick mat, frozen, as you go —
it takes the edge off the departure and gives the first ten minutes a shape. Walk
before you leave, not after you get back.

## What helps with genuine separation anxiety

**Make departures boring.** No emotional goodbye, no big greeting. Pick up your keys
fifty times a day and don't leave — break the association.

**Work out their actual threshold, then stay below it.** Film them. If they're fine
for four minutes and start at five, you practise at three. Not fifteen.

**Build up in small increments over weeks.** This is genuinely slow and the most
common mistake is going too fast.

**Company that feels like company.** A soft toy with a heartbeat mechanism helps some
dogs, particularly younger ones and recent rescues. Like everything else here it's a
comfort tool, not a cure.

**Get help.** Real separation anxiety is one of the hardest things to fix alone. A
qualified behaviourist, and sometimes your vet, will get you there faster.

## What doesn't help

Getting a second dog to fix the first one's anxiety — often you get two anxious dogs.
Punishing the mess. Crating a dog who panics in a crate. Leaving the radio on and
calling it a plan.

# ARTICLE 4

**Title:** Do Calming Coats Actually Work for Dogs? An Honest Answer
**Slug:** `do-calming-coats-work-for-dogs`
**Meta description:** Do anxiety wraps and calming coats really help dogs? What the
evidence says, who they work for, and how to introduce one properly.
**Publish:** September. **Target:** do calming coats work for dogs

---

Short answer: **for some dogs, noticeably. For others, not at all.** Anyone telling
you they work for every dog is selling something.

## The idea behind them

A calming coat applies gentle, constant pressure around the dog's torso — the same
principle as a weighted blanket, or swaddling an infant. Steady pressure appears to
have a settling effect on the nervous system for many animals, including us.

## What the evidence actually says

Research on pressure wraps for dogs is limited and mixed. Some studies find a
measurable reduction in stress markers like heart rate; others find effects no larger
than the dog simply being handled and attended to. There is no strong evidence that
they work for every dog, and there is no evidence they *treat* anxiety as a condition.

What there is a lot of is owner-reported improvement. That's worth something — it's
just not the same as clinical proof, and we're not going to present it as such.

## Who they seem to help most

Noise-related fear — fireworks, thunder, the vacuum — more than separation anxiety.
Dogs whose fear is acute and event-driven rather than constant. Dogs who already
enjoy being held or leaned on.

## How to introduce one properly

This is where most people go wrong.

1. Put it on for two minutes at home, while nothing is happening, with treats.
2. Build to ten minutes over several days.
3. Only once they're completely relaxed in it, use it during a mild trigger.
4. **Never** put it on for the first time during fireworks. A strange tight thing
   during a frightening event makes the event worse.

Fit matters: snug enough to apply pressure, loose enough to fit two fingers under.
Check it every 20 minutes and don't leave it on unsupervised or overnight.

## If it doesn't work

Take it off. Some dogs find the pressure aversive, and forcing it is
counterproductive. That's part of why we offer 30 days to send things back.

# ARTICLE 5

**Title:** Snuffle Mats and Lick Mats: What They're For and Why They Work
**Slug:** `snuffle-mats-lick-mats-explained`
**Meta description:** What snuffle mats and lick mats actually do, why sniffing and
licking calm dogs down, and how to use them properly.
**Publish:** August. **Target:** what is a snuffle mat for

---

Two of the cheapest things you can buy for an anxious dog are also two of the most
consistently useful. Here's why.

## Why licking calms dogs

Licking is a self-soothing behaviour. It's repetitive, it's rhythmic, and it releases
endorphins — which is why a stressed dog will often lick a paw, a blanket, or you.

A lick mat gives that behaviour somewhere useful to go. Spread something soft across
the textured surface — wet food, plain yoghurt, xylitol-free peanut butter, mashed
banana — and a thirty-second snack becomes fifteen minutes of steady, calming work.

**Freeze it.** This is the single tip most people miss. A frozen lick mat lasts three
or four times as long.

## Why sniffing calms dogs

A dog's nose takes up a vast amount of their brain. Using it is genuinely tiring in a
way that walking isn't — twenty minutes of scent work will settle a dog more reliably
than an extra twenty minutes on the lead.

A snuffle mat is a fleece mat with deep folds. Scatter dry food through it and your
dog has to work nose-first to find it. It turns dinner into an activity and it takes
the edge off the restlessness that leads to chewed skirting boards.

## When to use them

Before you go out. During fireworks or a storm. In the first weeks with a rescue.
While you're on a call. Instead of a bowl, on a rainy day. Before a vet visit.

## Using them safely

Supervise, especially at first, and especially with enthusiastic chewers. Wash them —
lick mats are usually dishwasher safe; snuffle mats machine wash but should be air
dried, since tumble drying wrecks the fleece strips. Count the food you put in them
as part of the daily allowance, not on top of it.

## What they won't do

They won't fix separation anxiety, and they won't stop a dog being frightened of
fireworks on their own. They're one part of a calmer environment, not a solution.
