### Lens: EFFECT-INFLATION — **REFUTED** → recommended CONTESTED

**Reasoning.** The claim welds two different papers into one. The inflation lens then destroys the "stable under publication-bias adjustment" property for the paper actually cited: the 2025 meta-analysis reports very strong evidence of publication bias and small-study effects, with PET–PEESE diverging catastrophically — PET d = 0.22, 95% CI [0.00, 6.22] in one analysis and PET d = 0.00, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00] in another, against implausible PEESE estimates of d = 8.45 and d = 9.11; the authors' own summary is that moderate evidence supports a SMALL effect with "publication bias, and small-study effects suggest[ing] potential inflation of effect sizes" [ESTABLISHED]. Twomey & Kroneisen's outcomes were "mostly assessed on the same day as the RCT experiment" [PROBABLE, from abstract snippet], meaning the 0.65 floor is a same-day word-list serial-recall number with no bearing on multi-week retention of technical prose. **Reasoning.** First, the citation is wrong in a way that destroys the claim's own validation plan [ESTABLISHED]: "13 RCTs, g = 0.65 [0.45, 0.85], I2 = 45.5%, robust to publication-bias adjustment, leave-one-out, setting, control condition, outliers and number of sessions, high risk of experimental bias because the vast majority did not report random sequence generation or allocation concealment" is verbatim Twomey & Kroneisen (2021, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology), not PubMed 40457944. Second, the boundary: the pooled effect is a serial-recall-of-unrelated-word-lists effect in university-setting adults, not a general learning effect — Roediger (1980) found mnemonics affect the ORDERING of recall far more than the number of items recalled irrespective of order, and Ondrej et al.'s own strongest evidence is explicitly immediate serial recall; Twomey & Kroneisen tested moderators for setting, control type, outliers and session count but NOT for material type or retention interval, and Ondrej et al. flag scarce data beyond one week. I could not find any RCT in this evidence base using dense propositional/technical prose or verbatim passages — the learner's stated priority material — so the g = 0.65 "floor" is unestablished, not merely uncertain, for the system's actual target (this is absence of supporting evidence, not evidence against). Third, the spatial mechanism is likely inert [PROBABLE]: Caplan, Legge, Cheng & Madan (2019) varied environments (apartment, open field, radial-arm maze) and found only small effects with navigation knowledge and ability not major determinants, concluding MoL may be a peg-method variant; Bouffard, Stokes, Kramer & Ekstrom (2018, Memory & Cognition) got comparable free-recall boosts from purely temporal scaffolds (autobiographical memories, making a sandwich); Caplan's lab (2022, Memory 30:7) found Body and Loci scaffolds equally superior, i.e. loci is not uniquely best. Fourth, transfer is the weak link: dual n-back beat MoL on transfer (Sci Rep 2021), and Gross et al. (2014, ACTIVE) found only ~25% of trained older adults showed follow-up behaviour consistent with MoL use even on the TRAINED task. I found no sign flip — no population or material where MoL reliably underperforms control — so this is not a refutation; it is a scope collapse from "core technique effect size floor" to "serial ordering of concrete list items, immediate test, lab setting, mechanism unattributable to the palace."

(3) DEMOTE THE PALACE, PROMOTE THE SCAFFOLD: Caplan 2019, Bouffard 2018 and Caplan 2022 jointly indicate the spatial/navigational component is not the active ingredient (body scaffolds, temporal scaffolds and peg systems match it). **New claims.**
