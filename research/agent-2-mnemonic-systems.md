## PART 1 — METHOD OF LOCI: EVIDENCE BASE

**1.1 [ESTABLISHED] MoL produces a medium, robust effect in RCTs.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of the method of loci (British Journal of Psychology, 2025; PubMed 40457944) pooled 13 randomised controlled trials and reported **g = 0.65, 95% CI 0.45–0.85**, stable under publication-bias adjustment, leave-one-out, control-condition, outlier and session-count sensitivity analyses. The same review flags **high risk of experimental bias** — most trials did not report random-sequence generation or allocation concealment. An earlier meta-analysis (Twomey & Kroneisen) reaches the same directional conclusion. So: real effect, mediocre trial hygiene, and a *medium* effect in short lab studies — not the 3–5× multiplier practitioners report from months of training. The gap between g = 0.65 and "26 → 62 words" is training volume.
→ Practice implication: treat published effect sizes as the floor for a 1-session intervention and Dresler-style volume as the realistic ceiling for 6 weeks of daily work.

**1.2 [ESTABLISHED] The Dresler 2017 benchmark is the single best-calibrated amateur target we have.** Dresler M, Shirer WR, Konrad BN, Müller NJ, Wagner IC, Fernández G, et al. *Mnemonic training reshapes brain networks to support superior memory.* Neuron 2017;93(5):1227–1235.e6. Mnemonics-naïve adults did **40 × 30-min sessions of method-of-loci training over 6 weeks** and went from recalling a mean of **26 of 72 words to 62 of 72**. Gains were still present at **4 months**. Design included a working-memory-training active control and a no-intervention control; only the MoL group showed the athlete-like network reorganisation, and *similarity to memory-athlete connectivity predicted retention at 4 months*. The athlete comparison group was 23 of the world's top-ranked competitors.
→ Practice implication: 20 hours of correctly-structured MoL practice is the empirically documented dose for a ~2.4× jump in list recall — make that the Week 1–6 contract.

**1.3 [ESTABLISHED] The follow-up replication/extension shows the gains are *durable*, not just larger.** Wagner IC, et al. *Durable memories and efficient neural coding through mnemonic training using the method of loci.* Science Advances 2021;7:eabc7606 (PubMed 33658191). Same 6-week design (memory training vs working-memory training vs no intervention) plus athlete comparison. Key behavioural finding: training specifically increased the proportion of **durable** memories (recalled at immediate free recall *and* after delay) rather than weak ones (immediate only). Neural signature: **activation decreases** in lateral prefrontal, parahippocampal and retrosplenial cortex in both athletes and post-training novices — i.e. *more efficient*, not harder-working, encoding — plus hippocampal–neocortical coupling during consolidation that scaled with durability.
→ Practice implication: score training runs on *delayed* recall (24h+), not immediate recall, because immediate recall does not distinguish the memories that survive.

**1.4 [ESTABLISHED] Expertise is strategic, not structural.** Both Dresler 2017 and Wagner 2021 find athletes differ from controls in distributed *functional connectivity* and *efficiency*, not in gross anatomy or general ability. This is the mechanistic warrant for the mission's core premise: the output is engineered.
→ Practice implication: never budget training time on "raw capacity" work; budget it on system-building and retrieval reps.

## PART 2 — JOURNEY CONSTRUCTION AND PALACE-PORTFOLIO MANAGEMENT

Everything in this section is practitioner-sourced. Label accordingly.

**2.1 [ANECDOTE] Loci density and room structure.** Practitioner consensus in the Art of Memory / Magnetic Memory literature: **~5 loci per room**, one item (or one chunk) per locus, loci walked in a fixed, non-ambiguous order, no branching, no loci that are visually confusable with each other (two identical chairs = collision). For verse work the standard instruction is one line/verse per locus with 5 loci per room. Sources located: artofmemory.com "Memory Palace Tips" and "How to Reuse Memory Palaces" (blocked to fetch, surfaced in search); magneticmemorymethod.com poetry and memory-palace guides.
→ Practice implication: build in 5-locus rooms so you can count loci arithmetically in the head during a timed run.

**2.2 [ANECDOTE] Portfolio size at competition level is large — hundreds to thousands of loci.** Dominic O'Brien is documented as using "hundreds, if not thousands" of locations across his Journeys; a WMC competitor reports needing thousands of loci **specifically because training volume, not competition, consumes them** — using 100 loci on one day meant those loci were unusable the next day or the day after, with **≥4 days** given for the encoded content to decay before reuse. Competitors keep several journeys in active rotation and cycle them.
→ Practice implication: size the portfolio off *training* consumption (loci used per session × cooldown days), not off the size of one competition event.

**2.3 [ANECDOTE] Ghosting / the "Ugly Sister Effect."** Old images resurfacing at a locus is the canonical reuse failure. Reported mitigations, in rough order of practitioner preference: (a) **rotation with cooldown** — most robust; (b) **overwrite with a stronger, more vivid image**, which reliably blots the old one; (c) **destroy/burn/shatter** the old image deliberately as a clearing ritual; (d) accept it — several sources state ghosting is not a serious problem *unless* the same journey is reused multiple times within a single day. Champions reportedly alternate between a small set of journeys for numbers/cards continuously without severe degradation.
→ Practice implication: run two parallel pools — a small "hot" pool of 2–4 journeys you deliberately overwrite daily for speed drills (where interference is acceptable because the content is disposable), and a large "cold" archive of one-use-per-topic journeys for material you actually intend to keep.

**2.4 [PROBABLE] Permanent storage and disposable storage are different jobs and should not share palaces.** This follows directly from 2.2–2.3 plus 1.3 (durable vs weak memories). Competition palaces are write/erase buffers; knowledge palaces are write-once archives that get retrieval-practised rather than overwritten.
→ Practice implication: never store exam/professional material in a journey that is also in your speed-drill rotation.

**2.5 [ANECDOTE] Indexing.** Practitioner-standard indexing schemes: a spreadsheet or note file with one row per palace (name, source location, room count, locus count, current occupant/topic, date last written, date free), plus an "entry hall" meta-palace whose loci point at other palaces via a symbol for each topic. Numbering loci globally (Palace 03, Room 2, Locus 4 → 03-2-4) lets you jump to an arbitrary position without walking the whole journey.
→ Practice implication: the portfolio spreadsheet is a Stage-2 deliverable, not an optional extra — an unindexed portfolio caps out around 10 palaces.

## PART 3 — NUMBER AND CARD SYSTEMS

**3.1 [ESTABLISHED, definitional] The families.**
- **Major system**: phonetic consonant→digit code; number → consonant skeleton → word. Deterministic and decodable; word choice is constrained by phonetics.
- **Dominic system** (Dominic O'Brien): digits → letters → **person's initials**, e.g. 15 = A.E. = Albert Einstein, with each person carrying a characteristic **action**. Free choice of person; less phonetic constraint, more arbitrary mapping.
- **PAO**: every 2-digit number owns a Person, an Action and an Object. Consuming 6 digits per locus (P from the first pair, A from the second, O from the third) triples throughput per locus.
- **Ben system** (Ben Pridmore): a 3-digit / 2-card compound system with ~1000 (or 2704 for 2-card) distinct single images. `[UNVERIFIED-THIS-SESSION]` — the specific image counts are from prior knowledge; only the system's existence and its position as an advanced alternative were confirmed in search (Art of Memory forum thread "What memory system do you use and why? Ben system / 2-digit major / 3-digit major / PAO").
- **3-digit systems (000–999)**: 1000 images, maximum throughput, brutal learning cost.

**3.2 [ANECDOTE] Recommended build order for a serious adult beginner: Major → 00–99 → 52-card → (only then) PAO or 3-digit.** Practitioner consensus surfaced in search: learn the Major first because it makes the 00–99 set *derivable rather than memorised* — you can reconstruct a forgotten image from the phonetic code — and because a Major-grounded PAO is less arbitrary than a free-choice PAO. Sources: magneticmemorymethod.com PAO guide; Art of Memory forum "MAJOR system VS PAO/DOMINIC and 00-99 VS 000-999".
→ Practice implication: build 00–99 on Major phonetics, not on free association, so the set is self-repairing when an image decays.

**3.3 [PROBABLE] The tradeoff is learning cost vs. loci-per-item, and it only pays above a throughput threshold.** 100 images buys 2 digits/locus (or 4–6 with pairing/PAO); 1000 images buys 3 digits/image and roughly 1.5× the digit rate of a 2-digit system for ~10× the image-learning cost. For a learner whose priority material is *text and concepts*, not competition digits, the 3-digit investment is close to pure waste.
→ Practice implication: for this mission's brief (retention of chosen study material), stop system-building at 00–99 + 52 cards and spend the saved 100+ hours on abstract-material conversion (Part 4) — build 3-digit only if competition becomes a real goal in year 2.

**3.4 [ESTABLISHED] Elite throughput, for calibration.** Wiederhold B. *An introduction to memory competitions, records and techniques.* arXiv:2504.06747. Best competitors memorise a **100-digit number in under 15 seconds**; the shuffled-deck record moved from **2 minutes (1993) to 12.74 seconds (Shijir-Erdene Bat-Enkh, 2018)**; results at competitions follow a power law over time; numbers are easier than playing cards; the paper's stated core mechanism is *association*, with images placed on a mental journey and decoded on the return walk. Companion preprint: *The speed of information processing at memory competitions* (bioRxiv 2025.03.31.646314) — short-task performance is **limited by reading speed**, and rate declines as a power law over longer durations.
→ Practice implication: past a certain point your bottleneck is intake speed, not encoding — which is why Agent 4's reading lane and this lane converge rather than compete.

**3.5 [ESTABLISHED] Official standards give clean, non-arbitrary gates.** International Master of Memory (WMSC): all 10 disciplines at approved championships, ≥3000 championship points, **1400 decimal digits in 1 hour**, **14 decks (728 cards) in 1 hour**, **one deck under 40 seconds** (worldmemorychampionships.com Millennium Standards; artofmemory.com wiki; en.wikipedia.org "Grand Master of Memory").
→ Practice implication: use the three numeric sub-standards as the terminal gates of the skill tree; they are externally defined and unfakeable.

**3.6 [ANECDOTE] Realistic amateur trajectory on cards.** First-ever attempt commonly **>6 minutes**; a practitioner who later set a USA record was at **1:27** a year before that record; half-deck (26 cards) at 1:00–1:15 and two half-decks back-to-back at 2:15–2:30 are reported as intermediate milestones. Recommended training load in the same sources: **4–5 sessions/week × 15–30 min**. Sources: themetalearners.com deck training schedule; Art of Memory forum "challenge to get below 2 minutes at speed cards"; tim.blog sub-60s piece.
→ Practice implication: 3-month realistic target is a full deck under 3 minutes, 12-month is under 90 seconds — not sub-60.

**3.7 [ANECDOTE] Realistic amateur trajectory on Memory League words.** Elite: 50 words in 32.41 s (Don Michael Vickers). Format: 60 s memorise / 4 min recall in order, 1 point per correct word. An intermediate practitioner reports a plateau around **15–18 words in 60 s**, finding >20 hard. Sources: Art of Memory forum "ALL TIME BEST Results: Competitive Scene: [Words]" and "Memorizing Words on Memory League"; artofmemory.com Memory League Words wiki.
→ Practice implication: 20 words/60 s is a genuinely good 6-month amateur number; anything claiming faster progress than that is selling something.

## PART 4 — LINK/STORY AND PEG SYSTEMS: WHAT SURVIVES ONCE YOU HAVE LOCI

**4.1 [PROBABLE] Link/story method is strictly inferior to loci for ordered lists but retains two niches.** Loci gives random access and error containment (one broken link ≠ total loss); chains fail catastrophically. Surviving niches: (a) **within-locus binding** — the 2–3 items sharing one locus must be chained into one interacting scene, which is exactly the link method operating at n=3; (b) **zero-setup capture** — a 6-item shopping list does not deserve a palace.
→ Practice implication: keep link/story as the *intra-locus* glue and as throwaway capture; never as the primary architecture for anything you intend to keep.

**4.2 [PROBABLE] Peg systems (rhyming, shape, alphabet) remain useful as *permanent numbered slots*, not as list storage.** Rhyming pegs (one-bun) and shape pegs (1-candle) are fast to acquire and give a fixed 1–10/1–20 index you can attach to *without* burning a palace — ideal for recurring small enumerations (the 7 items in a checklist you rewrite weekly). Alphabet pegs (A–Z) give a 26-slot index useful for alphabetically-keyed material. The Major-based 00–99 set is itself a 100-peg system and supersedes all of these for anything larger than ~20 items.
→ Practice implication: build rhyming pegs 1–10 in one sitting as a scratch register; do not invest in alphabet pegs unless your material is alphabetically organised.

## PART 5 — ABSTRACT AND CONCEPTUAL MATERIAL (highest-value section)

**5.1 [ESTABLISHED] Naïve MoL does *not* work on abstract nouns.** Roediger HL III (J Exp Psychol: Human Learning & Memory, 1980; PDF at psychnet.wustl.edu) compared mnemonic systems and found **no advantage for the mnemonic systems on abstract noun recall, while the loci method produced a reliable advantage with concrete nouns**. Corroborating framing appears across the imageability literature: MoL is specialised for high-imageability material because it is imagery-driven; the concreteness advantage is *larger* under MoL than under rote.
→ Practice implication: for abstract material the mnemonic is not "put it in a palace" — it is the *conversion step that precedes* the palace, and that step is where all the training time goes.

**5.2 [ESTABLISHED] Substitute-word / keyword conversion is the validated bridge.** Atkinson's keyword method (1975): pick a native-language word that *sounds like* the target, then form an interactive image linking keyword to meaning. Original result: **72% vs 46%** for keyword vs control on Russian vocabulary. Long-term retention is **[CONTESTED]** — the review literature reports growth after an initial decline in some studies (Lawson & Hogben 1998) and decline in others (Wang & Thomas 1995; Avila & Sadowski 1996). Sources located: ijllnet.thebrpi.org keyword-method review; epnet "Learning by the Keyword Mnemonic: Looking for Long-Term Benefits"; arXiv 2602.00762 (WordCraft, LLM-scaffolded keyword method).
→ Practice implication: keyword-encoded vocabulary must be handed to spaced retrieval (Agent 3's lane) immediately — the encoding wins the first exposure, the schedule wins the year.

**5.3 [PROBABLE] A *fixed personal symbol vocabulary* is what separates study-mnemonics from party tricks.** The recurring practitioner architecture (Mullen Memory's medicine/anatomy material; Magnetic Memory abstract-word guidance) is a stable, reused lexicon: one permanent image per recurring abstract term (e.g. "inhibition" → a specific padlock; "receptor" → a specific keyhole; "statute" → a specific stone tablet), so a new fact assembles from known parts rather than requiring fresh invention. Mullen's stated pipeline: brainstorm palaces → choose strong loci → convert information to images, with the palace *forcing chunking* as a side effect.
→ Practice implication: maintain a written symbol lexicon file with one row per abstract term and never re-invent an image for a term already in it — invention cost is the real bottleneck at scale, and the lexicon amortises it.

**5.4 [PROBABLE] Encode *relationships and structure*, not just items.** Items go on loci; relationships go into the **spatial and physical grammar** of the scene: hierarchy → vertical position or containment (parent contains children); sequence/process → direction of travel through the journey; causation → one image physically acting on the next; contrast → left/right or opposing rooms; magnitude → image size. This is standard practitioner architecture and is consistent with 5.1 (structure is not imageable, so it must be carried by the *arrangement*, which is).
→ Practice implication: before encoding a topic, decide its shape (list / tree / process / matrix) and pick the spatial grammar for that shape — the palace layout should be isomorphic to the content structure.

**5.5 [ANECDOTE] Animate, agentive imagery outperforms inanimate.** "Animacy and animate imagery improve retention in the method of loci among novice users," Memory & Cognition (2021), link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-021-01175-0 — retention improved among novice MoL users with animate imagery. (Abstract-level read only; treat as [PROBABLE] pending full text.)
→ Practice implication: default every symbol in the lexicon to a *creature or person doing something*, not an object sitting still.

**5.6 [CONTESTED] Do mnemonics support understanding and transfer, or only recall?**
- Against: "What They Remember May Not Be What They Understand: A Study of Mnemonic Recall and Performance by Introductory Statistics Students," *Journal of Statistics and Data Science Education* (2024), tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/26939169.2024.2334905 — students recalled mnemonics without corresponding conceptual performance. Review framing elsewhere: mnemonics "are not a panacea… should be used as an adjunct when information cannot be learnt conceptually."
- Also against: first-letter/acronym mnemonics specifically have **mixed** evidence — several studies show no benefit over control.
- For: Dresler 2017 / Wagner 2021 show far-reaching, durable *memory* gains; the medical-education literature reports mnemonics as effective for list-type content (cranial nerves) *in combination with* retrieval practice and spacing; and Noice & Noice's actor work (Part 6) shows meaning-based processing producing verbatim retention as a by-product — i.e. depth and verbatim are not opposed.
- Net position: **mnemonics reliably buy retrieval, and buy comprehension only when the image encodes the *mechanism*, not an arbitrary label.** An image of a padlock jamming a keyhole encodes inhibition-at-receptor; the acronym "SOAP" encodes nothing.
→ Practice implication: enforce a hard rule — an image is only acceptable if a naïve observer decoding the scene would recover the *relationship*, not merely the word; label-only images go to flashcards instead.

**5.7 [ESTABLISHED] Face–name is a three-component system and all three are load-bearing.** The face–name mnemonic = (a) **name transformation** to a concrete substitute ("Bryant" → "bride ant"), (b) a **prominent, distinctive facial feature**, (c) **interactive imagery** binding them. Research reports all three components essential; students using it outperformed their own preferred method on immediate *and* delayed tests; and **cue effectiveness of a facial feature decreases with how often that feature is used and increases with its distinctiveness across the face set**. Sources: PubMed 9237836 (*The Face–Name Mnemonic Strategy from a Different Perspective*); PubMed 7373246 (visual imagery mnemonic for face–name associations); escholarship.org/uc/item/4kg3m924; orbi.uliege.be name-learning review (2019).
→ Practice implication: rotate which feature class you key on (eyes/jaw/hairline/brow) across a room of people, because over-using one feature collapses its diagnosticity.

## PART 6 — VERBATIM TEXT

**6.1 [ESTABLISHED] Actors achieve verbatim retention as a by-product of meaning-based processing, not by rote.** Noice H & Noice T, *Elaborative memory strategies of professional actors*, Applied Cognitive Psychology 1992;6(5) (onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.2350060506); review: *The Expertise of Professional Actors*. Actors interrogate **why the character said these exact words**, generating goals, intentions and subtext; the resulting depth of processing yields word-for-word fidelity without line-by-line drilling. Retention is extreme — verbatim recall of roles **years** later (some cases at 3 years). Critically, **actors are not better at memorising in general**: given an encyclopedia passage they did not outperform non-actor students. This is trained, domain-specific strategy — exactly the mission's thesis.
→ Practice implication: for any passage you want word-perfect, first write one sentence per clause answering "why *this* word and not a synonym" — the fidelity comes from that, not from repetition count.

**6.2 [PROBABLE] Motor rehearsal and movement add measurable benefit.** Noice, Noice & Kennedy, *Learning dialogue with and without movement*, Memory & Cognition (link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196411) — dialogue learning compared with and without accompanying movement. The "active experiencing" training package (physical + emotional + intentional engagement) transferred to episodic memory gains in older adults, with effects persisting **4 months** (Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience 2017, 9:133).
→ Practice implication: rehearse target passages standing and moving with gesture attached to specific phrases; gesture becomes an additional retrieval cue independent of the semantic one.

**6.3 [ANECDOTE] The practitioner architecture for verbatim: one locus per line, one cue-word per line.** Standard instruction across the poetry-memorisation literature: **one verse/line per locus, 5 loci per room**, and for most lines **one well-chosen cue word is enough** — pick the word that reinstates the whole line, image it, place it. Function words and syntax ride on prosody and grammatical constraint rather than being separately encoded. Some practitioners explicitly blend **repetition + images** rather than choosing. Sources: Art of Memory forum threads "How to memorize text verbatim using mind palaces" (97047), "The Art of Memorizing Text Verbatim" (66267), "What is the best technique for memorizing sentences verbatim" (112148); magneticmemorymethod.com poetry material; thecraftofmemory.substack.com.
→ Practice implication: encode one image per line, then take a *spoken* pass at full speed — if a line stalls, the cue word was wrong, and you fix the cue rather than adding reps.

**6.4 [PROBABLE] Cadence and prosody carry a large share of verbatim load in metrical text.** Metre and rhyme constrain the search space at retrieval; this is why poetry is easier verbatim than prose of equal length, and it interacts with 6.3 (fewer images needed per line for metrical text).
→ Practice implication: always rehearse aloud at performance tempo — silent reading strips the prosodic cue channel that is doing much of the work.

**6.5 [ESTABLISHED] Competition format, for calibration.** The **poetry event exists at the USA Memory Championship, not the World Memory Championships** (removed internationally because it advantages English speakers). Format: a **previously unpublished poem, 15 min to memorise, 20 min to recall**, written out including title and author; **scoring is per-line all-or-nothing** — zero errors in a line scores its points, one or more errors scores zero. Sources: usamemorychampionship.com/events; artofmemory.com wiki Poetry Memorization Event; Art of Memory forum thread 26842.
→ Practice implication: train against all-or-nothing line scoring from day one — partial credit habits produce approximate recall, which is worth nothing for verbatim.

**6.6 [CONTESTED] First-letter / skeleton methods.** Evidence for first-letter mnemonics is mixed; several studies show no facilitation versus control. They function best as a **retrieval scaffold for already-learned material** (a first-letter skeleton of a memorised passage as a self-testing cue) rather than as an encoding method.
→ Practice implication: use first-letter skeletons as a compressed self-test sheet, never as the primary encoding.

## PART 7 — SKILL TREE: NOVICE → COMPETITION

Time-to-competence assumes the mission's 60–90 min/day. **Agent 5 owns scheduling — this is sequence and gates only, not a calendar.** All gates are on **24-hour delayed** recall unless marked "immediate", per §1.3.

### Stage 0 — Image Fluency (≈1 week)
- **Skill**: generate a vivid, animate, interacting image for an arbitrary concrete noun in <3 s.
- **Drills**: 50-word random-noun image sprints, timed; "make it move" pass (every static image gets an agent and a verb, §5.5).
- **Gate**: 50/50 nouns imaged in ≤150 s, self-rated vivid.
- **Failure mode**: images that are *labels* ("the word DOG floating") rather than scenes. Kills everything downstream.

### Stage 1 — First Palace + Link Glue (≈1–2 weeks)
- **Skill**: one 20-locus palace from a real, over-learned building; link method for within-locus binding.
- **Drills**: walk the palace forwards, backwards, and from locus 13 — with no content in it — until fluent; then 20-item concrete word lists.
- **Gate**: **20/20 concrete words, 2-min encode, 100% at 24 h**, and palace recited backwards without error.
- **Time**: 5–10 h. **Failure mode**: ambiguous/confusable loci (two identical chairs); loci in an order you have to *think* about.

### Stage 2 — Portfolio v1 + Index (≈2 weeks)
- **Skill**: 5 palaces × 20 loci = **100 indexed loci**; spreadsheet index with occupant/date-free columns (§2.5); hot-pool vs cold-archive split (§2.4).
- **Drills**: 40-item lists across 2 palaces; deliberate reuse experiment — write a list into palace A, wait 1 / 3 / 5 days, overwrite, measure ghosting rate. That measurement *is* your personal cooldown constant.
- **Gate**: **40/40 words, 4-min encode, ≥90% at 24 h**; measured personal cooldown documented (practitioner reference point: ~4 days, §2.2).
- **Time**: 10–15 h cumulative. **Failure mode**: building palaces faster than you can index them; portfolio becomes unusable past ~10.

### Stage 3 — Numbers: Major → 00–99 (≈4–6 weeks)
- **Skill**: instant bidirectional 00↔99 image mapping, Major-derived (§3.2).
- **Drills**: 10 images/day construction; flashcard drill both directions; 30-digit → 60-digit → 100-digit runs into palaces.
- **Gate**: **all 100 images recalled bidirectionally in ≤200 s total**, and **100 digits in 5 min at ≥95%** (immediate), **≥85% at 24 h**.
- **Time**: 25–40 h. **Failure mode**: free-associating the 00–99 set instead of deriving it from Major phonetics — the set then has no self-repair mechanism and decays permanently.

### Stage 4 — Cards (≈4 weeks, parallel with 3)
- **Skill**: 52 single-card images (Major-consistent where possible).
- **Drills**: quarter-deck → half-deck → full deck; time-boxed descending ladder.
- **Gate (3-month checkpoint)**: **full deck, 100% accurate, under 3 min** — calibrated against §3.6 (first attempt >6 min is normal).
- **Time**: 20–30 h. **Failure mode**: chasing speed before accuracy is 100%; sub-3-min with errors is worth zero under competition scoring.

### Stage 5 — Abstract Conversion + Symbol Lexicon (continuous from week 4; core competence by month 4–6)
- **Skill**: substitute-word conversion (§5.2), a written **symbol lexicon** (§5.3), and spatial grammars for list/tree/process/matrix (§5.4).
- **Drills**: take one page of real target material daily; classify its structure; convert; encode; **test at 24 h and 7 d**; log every failed image and *why* it failed. Lexicon grows by ~5 permanent symbols/session.
- **Gate**: **a 30-item conceptual set (definitions + relationships) at ≥85% at 7 days**, where scoring requires reproducing *the relationship*, not just the term (§5.6). Lexicon ≥150 entries.
- **Time**: 40–80 h; this is the largest single investment in the tree and the one that actually delivers the mission brief.
- **Failure mode**: label-only images that pass a 1-hour test and fail a 1-week transfer test; and re-inventing symbols for terms already in the lexicon.

### Stage 6 — Verbatim (month 4 onward)
- **Skill**: one-locus-per-line encoding + cue-word selection + active-analysis annotation (§6.1) + gestural/prosodic rehearsal (§6.2, §6.4).
- **Drills**: 12-line poem → 30-line → 60-line, each with a written "why this word" annotation pass; spoken full-tempo recall; first-letter skeleton as self-test only (§6.6).
- **Gate**: **a 30-line unfamiliar poem in 15 min, ≥60% of lines perfect under all-or-nothing line scoring at 24 h** (USAMC format, §6.5). Elite competitors score far higher; 60% is a realistic 6–9 month amateur mark.
- **Failure mode**: silent rehearsal (strips prosody); encoding whole lines as images instead of selecting one cue word.

### Stage 7 — Compound Systems (month 9+, **only if** competition is a real goal)
- **Skill**: PAO (6 digits/locus) or 2-card compound; optionally 000–999 (§3.1, §3.3).
- **Gate to *enter*** this stage: Stage 3 and 4 gates held for 30 consecutive days with no re-learning; otherwise the 900 new images will cannibalise time that Stage 5 needs.
- **Gate to exit**: **200 digits in 5 min at ≥95%**; **deck under 90 s at 100%** (12-month realistic amateur target, §3.6).
- **Failure mode**: building 1000 images for a use case that never materialises — the single most common wasted year in this hobby.

### Stage 8 — Competition Calibration (year 2)
- **Terminal external gates** (§3.5): **1400 digits / 1 h**, **14 decks / 1 h**, **one deck < 40 s** = International Master of Memory sub-standards. Elite reference points: 100 digits < 15 s; deck WR 12.74 s (2018); ML Words 50 in 32.41 s.
- **Failure mode**: mistaking competition throughput for the mission's actual objective (durable knowledge). Stage 7–8 are optional; Stages 0–6 are the deliverable.

### 12-week composite target (matches mission spec)
Dresler-equivalent or better on the standard measure — **≥55/72 words** at the trained format — plus Stage 2, 3 and 4 gates cleared, a ≥150-entry symbol lexicon, and 100 indexed loci in an indexed portfolio.
