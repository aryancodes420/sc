## 1. The only RCT-grade dose evidence: Dresler et al. 2017

**[ESTABLISHED] [SECONDARY-RETRIEVAL]** Dresler, Shirer, Konrad et al. (2017), *Neuron* 93(5):1227–1235.e6. Design: 51 memory-naive adults randomised to three arms — (a) method-of-loci mnemonic training, (b) short-term/working-memory training (a Concentration-like sequence task, i.e. an *active* control), (c) no-training control. Training was home-based, **up to 30 min/day, ~40 days / 6 weeks**, after a single in-person instruction day. Outcome: free recall of a 72-word list.

Numbers returned:
- Loci group: **~26 → ~62 of 72 words** recalled.
- Gain by arm: loci **+35 words**, short-term-memory training **+11**, no-training **+7**. (Note the internal tension between "26→62" (+36) and "+35"; these are different framings/subsets in secondary reporting. Treat the loci gain as **~+35 words**, i.e. roughly a doubling-plus.)
- Reference ceiling: 23 world-class memory athletes recalled **70.8 ± 0.6** vs matched controls **39.9 ± 3.6** of 72 words at 20-min delay.
- **Durability: gains persisted at four-month follow-up with no continued training.**
- fMRI: training-induced functional connectivity changes correlated with the athlete-vs-control network signature.

Sources retrieved: sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170308131236.htm; ucrisportal.univie.ac.at listing; sciencenews.org/article/brain-training-turns-recall-rookies-memory-masters; psychologytoday.com/us/blog/brain-waves/201703/how-train-your-brain-memory-champion.

Three design points that matter more than the headline:
1. The **active control (working-memory drilling) gained +11 — about a third of the mnemonic arm.** Time-on-task is not the active ingredient; strategy is.
2. **30 min/day was the ceiling of the protocol, not a floor.** There is no RCT showing 60–90 min/day beats 30. Our program's 60–90 min/day is an *extrapolation beyond the evidence base*, justified by breadth of material, not by proven dose-response.
3. **Four-month durability without practice** is the single strongest argument for front-loading technique acquisition hard and early: the skill does not evaporate the way fitness does.

→ **Practice implication:** Guarantee the first 6 weeks hit ≥30 min/day of *strategy* work with an instruction phase up front; treat everything above 30 min/day as unproven surplus allocated to real target material, not to more drilling.

## 2. What the top practitioners actually do

### Joshua Foer — 1 year, novice → USA champion
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Trained **"just half an hour every day,"** in his parents' basement, wearing **blacked-out safety goggles and earmuffs**, memorising dog-eared yearbooks (Scotsman interview, scotsman.com/arts-and-culture/interview-joshua-foer-memory-mapping-champ-1679131).
- **[CONTESTED]** The widely repeated "an hour a day" figure did not surface in any first-person source I retrieved. **The documented figure is ~30 min/day.** This matters: the most famous novice-to-champion case ran on roughly the Dresler dose.
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Coached by Ed Cooke; **logged progress on a spreadsheet**; consulted Cooke regularly. Cooke's interventions were *environmental and motivational* (earmuffs, pinhole glasses to kill distraction when bored), not just technical (shortform.com/blog/joshua-foer-moonwalking-with-einstein/; artofmemory.com/blog/moonwalking-with-einstein-joshua-foer/).
- **[DOCUMENTED]** The **OK-plateau breaker**: metronome set **10–20% faster than his current pace**, practised at that speed until errors stopped; every troublesome card **analysed for why it failed**. Card times resumed falling "within a couple of days."
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Result: won 2006 USA speed cards, **52 cards in 1:40**.

→ **Practice implication:** Copy the exact mechanism — a pace forcing-function set 10–20% above current best, plus a written post-mortem on every failed item — rather than copying hours.

### Alex Mullen — the highest-leverage template for our learner
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Trains **~30 minutes/day on average**, allocated **~25% speed drills / ~75% full competition-event practice** (mullenmemory.com/alex-mullen-memory-sports, retrieved via search).
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Training philosophy: once a sufficient foundation exists, **push speed and trust accuracy to follow** — the inverse of the intuitive "be accurate first, then faster."
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Anki structure: three parent decks (Medicine, Languages, Memory Sports) with subdecks; card template with four fields — Front, Back, **Big Picture** (conceptual scaffolding), **Mnemonic** (description of the image in the palace). He runs a small constricted memory-sports deck **daily, "a few minutes"** (mullenmemory.com/memory-palace/my-anki-setup).
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Session discipline: Pomodoro — focused block, ~5 min break, longer ~15 min break after several cycles.
- **[SIGNIFICANT]** Mullen was a **full-time medical student** while world champion on ~30 min/day of memory-sport training. The load is compatible with a demanding day job because the *learning* volume lives in Anki against real material, and the *sport* volume is small and speed-focused.

→ **Practice implication:** Split the daily budget the Mullen way — a small, fast, timed drill block (~25%) plus a larger block applying the system to real target material (~75%) — and keep a permanent mnemonic field on every card so image and fact stay coupled.

### Nelson Dellis — the volume end of the distribution
- **[DOCUMENTED/interview]** Historically **4–5 hours/day** of sets (numbers, cards, names, words); pulled back over time; **ramps to 3–5 h/day** approaching competition; has reported **up to 6 h/day on retention drills** (magneticmemorymethod.com/extreme-memory-improvement-nelson-dellis/; mindhack.com/episode/nelson-dellis; findingmastery.com/podcasts/nelson-dellis/; thisisyourbrain.com/2024/05/a-memory-workout-with-nelson-dellis/).
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Day shape: wakes **just before 6 a.m.**, work, **gym 1–1.5 h**, then memory training.
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Week shape: **discipline pairing across the week — Mon/Wed, Tue/Thu, Fri — with one weekend day reserved for experimenting and working on systems.** This is the single most transferable structural idea in his account: *system change is quarantined to a designated slot; the other days are execution only.*
- **[DOCUMENTED]** His recommendation for non-athletes: **~15 minutes/day** (cnbc.com/2020/01/09/usa-memory-champion-nelson-dellis-how-to-improve-your-memory).
- **[CLAIMED]** Reported day structure is "chunks of the day focused on one discipline, drills and mock sets" — plausible and consistent across interviews but I found no published minute-by-minute session log or documented warm-up protocol. **Anyone who tells you Dellis's exact warm-up is inventing it.**

→ **Practice implication:** Adopt the block-and-quarantine week — fixed discipline pairs on fixed days, and exactly one weekly slot in which changing your system is permitted.

### Boris Konrad — athlete *and* the study author
- **[DOCUMENTED]** As researcher: his protocols are the 6-week, **≤30 min/day at home after one instruction day** design; he reports participants "more than doubled" memory performance (boriskonrad.com/en/memory-training; labroots.com/trending/neuroscience/5636/training-brain-memory-athlete).
- **[DOCUMENTED]** Records include **215 names matched in 15 minutes**.
- **[NOT FOUND]** No documented figure for his own daily training hours surfaced. Do not assert one.
- **[ESTABLISHED]** In the athlete cohort, **all athletes attributed superior memory to deliberate training in mnemonic strategies**, not to innate ability — and the athletes showed no structural brain anomaly.

### Simon Reinhard, Ben Pridmore, Dominic O'Brien
- **[DOCUMENTED-thin]** No specific daily-hour figures were returned for any of the three. What is documented: O'Brien's origin (1987, after seeing Creighton Carvello memorise a deck) and his 54-decks single-sighting record; Pridmore's 2009 world title and consecutive-win streak; Reinhard's 2010 record (memorise.org/memory-champions/…; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Pridmore; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominic_O%27Brien).
- **[CLAIMED]** "Thousands of hours of training" appears in secondary write-ups (worldmemorychampionships.com). That is promotional framing, not a training log.
- **Honest conclusion: the field's documented daily doses cluster at ~30 min/day (Foer, Mullen, Konrad's protocol) with one documented high-volume outlier (Dellis). Anyone citing "champions train 4 hours a day" as the norm is generalising from Dellis.**

→ **Practice implication:** Set the program's *core* mnemonic dose at 30 focused minutes and treat additional time as application volume; do not sell 4 h/day as the elite standard, because the documented record does not support it.

## 3. Sustainable review volume (what breaks)

- **[PROBABLE] [SECONDARY-RETRIEVAL]** Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen (2015), PubMed 26498443: survey of 72 medical students; in multivariate regression, **each additional ~1,700 unique Anki cards seen, or ~445 practice MCQs, was associated with +1 point on USMLE Step 1**, controlling for MCAT and prior honours. Read this correctly: it is (i) correlational, (ii) about *unique cards seen*, not reviews, and (iii) a brutally steep price — thousands of cards per exam point. **Volume buys real but expensive returns.**
- **[PROBABLE]** Associated literature retrieved (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10176558; link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40670-023-01826-8): daily Anki use correlated with higher Step 1 scores, **consistency of use** predicted scores and perceived retention better than raw volume, and one study reported an association with better sleep quality but no broader wellness difference.
- **[ANECDOTE] [CLAIMED]** Practitioner/commercial guidance converges on: ~100–150 reviews/day (15–25 min) sustainable long-term for most; ~200–250/day (25–35 min) sustainable during exam sprints for a minority; **300+/day (40+ min) sustainable for well under a fifth of users and effectively only for full-time students** (smartrecallai.com/blog/daily-review-load-management; studycardsai.com blogs). These are vendor blogs — treat the *shape* of the curve as informative and the exact percentages as marketing.
- **[ANECDOTE]** Documented community failure pattern: >50 new cards/day drives review load toward 400+/day; being **"buried"** by a 1,000–2,000-card backlog is the reported trigger for abandonment, and attempting to clear it in one sitting fails.

→ **Practice implication:** Cap steady-state at ~150 reviews/day (~20 min), derive the new-card cap from that (roughly new/day ≈ reviews/day ÷ 10 at mature intervals), and treat any sustained load over ~250/day as a scheduled sprint with an end date, never as a default.

## 4. Deliberate practice: the real definition and the honest critique

**[ESTABLISHED] Ericsson's definition** — highly structured activity designed specifically to improve performance, requiring (1) a specific, well-defined goal, (2) full concentration, (3) immediate informative feedback, (4) operating outside the comfort zone at the edge of current ability, with repetition-plus-refinement. It is explicitly *not* inherently enjoyable (frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full; producttalk.org glossary; fs.blog/deliberate-practice-guide/).

**[ESTABLISHED] The OK-plateau mechanism**: repetition improves performance only until the skill becomes **automatic**; past automaticity, further repetition consolidates the current level rather than improving it. Continued improvement requires deliberately re-introducing cognitive control — i.e. breaking automaticity by raising demands (Ericsson, as above; Foer's metronome is the applied case).

**[ESTABLISHED] Dose ceiling**: Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) — the best violinists practised **~4 h/day**, accumulating **>10,000 h by age 20**, ~2,500 h more than "good" violinists and ~5,000 h more than the teacher group. Secondary syntheses of practice-duration studies report **little benefit beyond ~4 h/day, with returns declining after ~2 h** (retrieved via royalsocietypublishing.org/rsos/article/6/8/190327 and bulletproofmusician.com/how-many-hours-a-day-should-you-practice/ — the declining-returns figure is **[CONTESTED]/[CLAIMED]**, from a secondary synthesis, not a single primary trial).

**[ESTABLISHED] The critique.** Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), *Psychological Science* 25:1608–1618: meta-analysis across domains found deliberate practice explained **26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, <1% in professions** (journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614535810). Conclusion: practice matters and is not close to sufficient; the 10,000-hour popularisation overstates it by a wide margin. The debate continued (Ericsson & Harwell 2019 defending the original definition; Macnamara & Hambrick 2020) — the disagreement is substantially about *what counts as* deliberate practice and how it's measured, not about whether practice matters.

**Crucially for us: memory sport sits in the "games" end (26%), the high end of the DP-explains-variance range — highly structured, immediately scorable, low luck.** That is the most favourable domain profile for a training program. But the education/professions numbers (4% / <1%) are the honest ceiling on what memory drilling alone will do for real-world professional outcomes.

→ **Practice implication:** Structure every block to satisfy all four DP conditions (numeric goal, timer, immediate scoring, edge-of-ability difficulty) — and promise elite results only on the *scored memory tasks*, where the variance-explained evidence is strong.

## 5. Transfer: what we may not promise

- **[ESTABLISHED]** Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme (2016), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(4):512–534 — meta-analysis of **87 publications / 145 experimental comparisons** of working-memory training. Reliable near/intermediate transfer to verbal and visuospatial working memory immediately post-training; **no convincing evidence of far transfer** to nonverbal ability, verbal ability, word decoding, reading comprehension, or arithmetic when compared against *treated* control groups. Conclusion: repetitive computerised WM practice is unlikely to yield generalised cognitive benefits (journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616635612).
- **[ESTABLISHED]** Ramon, Miellet et al. (2016), *PLOS One* 11(3):e0150972, "Super-Memorizers Are Not Super-Recognizers": two world-championship-level memorizers with face-name world records **excelled at associative face-name learning but were as impaired as controls on face inversion and the other-race effect**, and showed **no increased hippocampal volume**. The trained gain was in the associative-strategy layer; the underlying perceptual system was untouched (journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0150972).
- **[ESTABLISHED]** Dresler 2017's own athlete data: no structural brain differences; athletes attribute performance to strategy training.

**Synthesis:** the gains are **strategy-specific and material-specific**. We are installing an encoding-and-retrieval *procedure*, not raising general cognitive ability. The 4-month durability result says the procedure sticks; the transfer literature says it will not make the learner globally smarter.

→ **Practice implication:** Write the promise as "near-perfect recall of material you deliberately encode with the system," and explicitly disclaim IQ/working-memory/general-cognition gains in the program's stated goals so expectation failure cannot become a quit trigger.

## 7. THE FIVE REASONS PEOPLE PLATEAU OR QUIT — with implementable rules

**1. Automaticity plateau ("OK plateau").** Performance becomes automatic; further repetition consolidates rather than improves. **[ESTABLISHED]** (Ericsson; Foer's applied case.)
> **Rule:** Every speed block runs a timer set to **90% of current best time** (i.e. 10–20% faster pace). If accuracy holds ≥90% for two consecutive sessions at that pace, the target ratchets down again automatically. Never practise at comfortable pace outside the 5-min warm-up.

**2. Review-debt spiral.** New-card intake outruns review capacity; backlog reaches the "buried" threshold; the learner attempts a heroic clear, fails, and abandons. **[ANECDOTE→PROBABLE]** (Anki community failure pattern; sustainability curves.)
> **Rule:** Hard cap ~150 reviews/day. **Automatic circuit-breaker: if backlog exceeds 2× the daily cap on any day, new cards go to 0 until backlog clears, and the backlog is drained in capped daily chunks (filtered deck) — never in one sitting.** Backlog is a scheduling event, not a moral failure.

**3. Technique maximalism / system churn.** Endless re-tooling of the system (new PAO, new palaces, new software) substitutes for practice; the learner never accumulates reps on any one system, so scores never move. **[PROBABLE]** — inferred from Dellis's documented quarantine of "working on systems" to one weekend day, and from Ericsson's requirement of repetition-with-refinement of a *fixed* task.
> **Rule:** **System freeze.** Changes to encoding systems, palace inventories, or tooling are permitted only in one designated weekly slot, and any adopted change must survive a 2-week trial with logged scores before a further change is allowed.

**4. Transfer disappointment ("I can memorise cards but it didn't help my actual work").** The learner trains synthetic material, sees no change in professional performance, concludes the whole thing is a parlour trick, and stops. **[ESTABLISHED]** that gains are strategy- and material-specific (Melby-Lervåg 2016; Ramon 2016), and **[ESTABLISHED]** that DP explains only ~4% of variance in education and <1% in professions (Macnamara 2014) — so the disappointment is *predictively correct* if training is synthetic-only.
> **Rule:** **≥50% of every week's encoding volume must be real target material** (the actual books, cases, curriculum), never synthetic decks. Program goals are written as domain outcomes ("recall X protocol on demand"), and the program **explicitly disclaims general-intelligence gains up front** so the expectation is never formed.

**5. Habit fragility in the acquisition window.** The behaviour is abandoned before automaticity is reached, and a single missed day is misread as failure. **[ESTABLISHED]** Lally et al. (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology*: 96 volunteers, 12 weeks; time to 95% of asymptotic automaticity **median 66 days, range 18–254**; **missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation** (onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674).
> **Rule:** Fix one **time and one physical context** for the core block and never move it during the first 66 days. Adherence is scored on a **7-day rolling average, not on streaks**; a single missed day is explicitly defined as a non-event (evidence-backed), while **two consecutive misses trigger an automatic dose reduction to a 15-minute minimum session** (Dellis's own recommendation for non-athletes) rather than a skip — the session floor never goes to zero.

**Sixth, honourable mention — coach/feedback absence.** Foer's documented setup included a coach (Ed Cooke) reviewing his logged spreadsheet and prescribing environmental fixes when boredom set in **[D]**; DP requires immediate informative feedback **[ESTABLISHED]**. A solo learner with no scored log has neither.
> **Rule:** The numeric log is mandatory and non-optional; if no human coach exists, the weekly benchmark plus written error autopsies serve as the feedback channel.
